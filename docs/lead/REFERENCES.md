# GOPALA — sourced reference for design and code

A reference for a calm, deterministic settlement game set in the Kathmandu Valley in the age of the Gopala (cowherd) kings. It keeps three things apart: what a chronicle says, what has been dug up or measured, and what the game has to make up. All links were opened on 8 October 2026. Page and folio numbers come from the scanned text and nothing is quoted from memory. Where something is unknown, the doc says so.

---

## 1. Evidence labels

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[Archaeological]** | Excavation, dated object, sediment core, lab date, or archaeobotany. |
| **[Inscription]** | Stone text from its own time. In this valley inscriptions start in the Licchavi period (2nd century CE onward), never with the Gopalas. |
| **[Chronicle tradition]** | A vamshavali or purana. Written late and often legendary. |
| **[Later-period analogy: X]** | Documented, but for a named later period: Licchavi (c. 2nd–9th c. CE), Malla (c. 13th–18th c.), Shah / 19th century. |
| **[Ethnographic, modern]** | Present-day practice, a modern survey, or modern instrument data. |
| **[Gameplay invention]** | Our own rule. |

Working rule: herding, the monsoon, and grain grown on wet valley ground can be played as if early, provided each carries its real label. Tiered temples, maize, potatoes, chilli, and named festivals cannot appear in the first era unless the text says they are later.

## 2. What is actually known about the Gopala / Mahishapala era

**The chronicle.** [Chronicle tradition] The *Gopalarajavamsavali* is a Newari/Sanskrit chronicle compiled in the time of Jayasthiti Malla (late 14th c.). Cecil Bendall found it in the Durbar Library in 1898–99, and the National Archives in Kathmandu now holds it. Vajracharya and Malla edited and translated it (1985) [1]. Folio 17 says the valley was first a dense forest where sages lived. The Gopalas then came "from Malakha to Gograma". A brown cow named Bahuhri, owned by a cowherd named Nepa, let her milk flow into a hole by the Bagmati every day. When people dug there, Pashupati appeared. Eight Gopala kings follow, from Bhumigupta to Jinagupta, with reigns of 34–91 years each. Then come three Mahishapala (buffalo-herd) kings and the Kiratas [1]. Kamal P. Malla's introduction says Abhira (cowherd-clan) rulers are real in the 6th–7th c. CE. He calls the chronicle's placement of them before the Kiratas "nothing if not dubious" [1].

**A second, different chronicle.** [Chronicle tradition] Wright's 1877 translation of a Parbatiya vamshavali (pp. 107–109) tells it another way [2]. Cowherds arrive with Krishna after he drains the valley. They build cowsheds, and a cow named Ne reveals the buried light of Pashupati. Ne Muni then crowns the cowherd's son, **Bhuktamana**. Eight rajas reign for "521 years", live near Mata Tirtha, and "tend Brahmans' cows". After them comes "an Ahir, from the plains of Hindustan". The two chronicles give different king names, reign lengths, and totals. So the "dynasty" has no single version.

**Dating.** There is no Gopala inscription, coin, or excavated layer, and no reliable date. The reign lengths are formulaic and should not be added up into a calendar. [Inscription] The earliest dated stone from the valley is the life-size (171 × 49 cm) Jaya Varman statue, found at Maligaon in 1992. Its year 107 is usually converted to 185 CE, but readings differ: Garbini proposed 207 / 285 CE, and Tiwari offers another reckoning that also gives c. 185/186 [3][4]. That already puts us in the Licchavi horizon.

**Earliest settlement.** [Archaeological] At the Satya Narayan site in Hadigaon (Handigaon), Italian (IsMEO) and Department of Archaeology teams dug in 1984–88. They found brick foundations, walls, and paving dating from the 1st c. BCE to the 10th c. CE. Tiwari cites a radiocarbon range of **167 BCE – 1 CE** for the earliest wall, quoting Verardi 1988, p. 181 [4]. An earlier dig (Deo, 1965, Manamaneswor) reached only the early centuries CE [5]. A Durham-led survey treats Handigaon as a possible pre-Licchavi and Licchavi centre and mapped buried brick footings and stone drains [6]. This doc relies on Tiwari's citation of Verardi; the 1992 final report was not re-read.

**Lake legend vs geology.** [Chronicle tradition] In Buddhist tradition (the *Swayambhu Purana*), Manjushri cuts the southern hill (Chobhar) with a sword to drain the lake. Hindu versions give the role to Krishna [7][1]. Malla notes that the *Gopalarajavamsavali* itself skips the draining story and simply starts with forest [1]. [Archaeological] The real lake (Paleo-Kathmandu Lake) formed about 1 million years ago when the proto-Bagmati was dammed. Its water dropped sharply around 48 and 38 thousand years ago, probably because of earthquakes, and the last remnant was gone by about 12,000 years ago [8]. The valley was dry land some 10,000 years before any chronicle king. **Design use:** present the lake as a story the settlers tell (label it). Do not simulate it as an event.

## 3. Settlement and architecture

**Defensible for an early look:**
- **Brick and timber.** [Archaeological] Coursed brick existed at Hadigaon by the turn of the era [4]. [Inscription] A 610 CE Amshuvarman record uses *istaka* (brick) and *panktita* (laid in courses) [4].
- **Water.** [Inscription] Tiwari dates the Satya Narayan sunken spout (*hiti*) in Hadigaon to **550 CE**, the oldest still working. Licchavi texts call a canal *tilamaka* and the canal-fed conduit *pranali-jaladroni*. He argues the system is older (Kirata) but offers no dig as proof. He also argues that wells were never the main supply on ridge-top settlements, which used rain-fed ponds at the top of the settlement, sometimes fed by canals [9]. A plain pond → runnel → spout → drain is the honest early form. Carved makara spouts (e.g. Manga Hiti) are the mature Licchavi-and-later version.
- **Ridge-top siting.** Tiwari hypothesises that early towns sat on ridges for drainage [9]. Treat that as a scholar's hypothesis, not a finding.
- **Cowsheds.** [Chronicle tradition] Cowsheds are the only building Wright's chronicle gives the cowherds [2].

**Anachronistic for a Gopala era:**
- **Tiered temples, tile roofs, carving, gilding.** [Later-period analogy: Malla] UNESCO describes tiered temples of fired brick, mud mortar, and timber with "small overlapping terracotta tiles", carved windows and struts, and gilded brass. It says this tradition came "truly into its own" in **1500–1800** [10].
- **Dachi apa** glazed facing brick. Tiwari says it appears in walls only from the Malla period [4].

**Stupas.** [Chronicle tradition] The link between Chabahil (Charumati/Dhando) and Ashoka's daughter is legend. Tiwari argues the "charuvati" inscribed brick is a reused Malla-period brick and finds nothing proving the dome predates the Malla period [4]. UNESCO calls Swayambhu the valley's oldest stupa but gives no date [10]. A white dome on a far hill is acceptable if it carries the label.

**Thatch and roofs.** Thatch is **[Gameplay invention]**: no excavated early roof was found for this doc.

## 4. Cattle and pastoral life

**Ancient evidence is thin.** [Chronicle tradition] The cowherd identity, a miracle cow, cowsheds, and "tending Brahmans' cows" [1][2] tell us nothing about herd size, breed, dairying, or ploughing.

**Licchavi.** [Inscription] Licchavi edicts and land grants often have a reclining bull carved at the top (e.g. Regmi nos. 93, 95, 101, 118) [11], which works as a visual for a boundary stone. Amshuvarman's Gairidhara grant (no. 93, early 7th c.) rewards villagers for building the Nilishala canal. The online summary reads the reward as barring entry by an official office and by (requisitioned?) loaded horses, uncastrated bulls, and carts [11]. That suggests draught animals were part of state levies, but a Sanskritist should confirm it. An Amshuvarman grant from near Tistung (no. 85) resettles a village because of *mahatī mahiṣīpīḍā*. Literally that is "great affliction by she-buffaloes", but the meaning is **unverified** [11].

**Malla.** [Later-period analogy: Malla] The *Gopalarajavamsavali* counts animals as wealth. In NS 454 (c. 1334 CE) a fire at Bode killed 24 people, 46 buffaloes, 116 cows and calves, and 58 sheep and goats. In NS 496 (c. 1376 CE) the palace fined two herdsmen 10 *pala* because their buffaloes trampled fields, and half the fine went to the royal priest [1]. A herd that strays into crops and brings a fine is a fully documented loop, but it is 14th-century evidence.

**18th–19th c.** [Later-period analogy: Shah] Kirkpatrick (visited 1793; published 1811, pp. 149–150) found the cattle no better than Bengal's. He thought their milk was among the richest anywhere, but noted that the inhabitants "did not seem to have any to spare" [12]. Wright (1877, p. 46) wrote that there were "few cattle in the valley, as there is no grazing ground except at the foot of the hills", that farm work was done "almost entirely by hand", and (p. 47) that buffaloes, sheep and goats for food were imported. On p. 45 he notes that killing a cow was punished by death [2]. **Design implication:** the valley floor is for grain and the herd lives on the hill edge. Milk is scarce and precious, not a bulk export.

**Fodder seasonality.** [Ethnographic, modern] A 1998–99 survey of five mid-hill villages (not in the valley) found grazing and cut grass dominating the rains, with tree fodder and crop residues taking over in the dry season. By share of crop-residue fodder: rice straw 48%, maize 24%, millet 6%, wheat 3%. Over 90% of households reported a deficit in the late dry season (March–May visits), against 21% in September [13]. In a Gopala setting, replace maize straw with millet or barley straw (see §5).

**Sacred cow.** [Ethnographic, modern] The cow is Nepal's national animal under the Constitution of 2015, Art. 9(3) [14]. On Gai Tihar the cow is fed and worshipped [15]. The death penalty for cow-killing is 19th-century [2]. None of this proves an ancient ban. Cow slaughter as a mechanic is a reviewer question (§10).

**Gai Jatra.** [Later-period analogy: Malla / living Newar practice] The schoolbook story credits Pratap Malla (r. 1641–74). Historian Dinesh Raj Pant says no written proof exists. Gautama V. Vajracharya argues the rite is older than the Mallas and that commemorating the dead is a "second-layer" development [16]. One word in the *Gopalarajavamsavali* glossary is glossed "the cow-festival", but the matching passage could not be identified, so no 14th-c. witness is claimed [1].

## 5. Agriculture and crop calendar

**The central gap:** no archaeobotanical data from the Kathmandu Valley was found. Nobody has published what the earliest valley farmers grew.

**Antiquity.** [Archaeological] Rice was part of Ganges-plain economies by c. 7000–6000 BCE, as foraging and early cultivation, with paddy-field systems coming later [22]. That is regional context, not valley proof. High-altitude Mustang has a 3,000-year crop record, but its climate is not Kathmandu's (Knörzer 2000; only the abstract was seen) [24]. [Inscription] By the 7th century Licchavi grants measure endowments in *taṇḍula-mānikā* (husked rice) and *dhānya-mānikā* (paddy/grain) (Regmi no. 118), so rice is documented in Licchavi times [11].

**Modern Hills calendar.** [Ethnographic, modern] Read from FAO CFSAM 2024, Figure 9, which draws on MoAD data. Kathmandu falls in the Mid Hills zone. FAO says double rice cropping is limited to land below 800 m, so the valley (about 1,300 m) gets **one rice crop** a year [18].

| Crop (Hills) | Sow / transplant | Growing | Harvest |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paddy (summer) | May–Jun (Jestha–Asar) | Jul–Aug | Sep–Oct (Asoj–Kartik) |
| Millet (relay crop after maize; rainfed) | Jun–Jul | Aug–Sep | Oct–Nov |
| Wheat (rainfed) | Oct–Dec (Kartik–Poush) | Jan–Feb | Mar–May (Chaitra–Jestha) |
| Barley (rainfed) | Oct–Dec | Jan–Feb | Mar–Apr |
| Lentils (as charted for irrigated Hills; looks odd, needs local check) | Feb–Mar | Apr–Sep | Oct–Nov |
| *Maize (rainfed), modern only* | Mar–Apr | May–Jul | Aug–Sep |

FAO's text adds that irrigated lower terraces and riverbeds grow rice followed by wheat or rapeseed (mustard), while rainfed upland grows maize [18]. Nepali months begin around the middle of each Gregorian month: Baisakh in mid-April, then Jestha, Asar, Shrawan (the fiscal year starts on **16 July**, i.e. 1 Shrawan [18]), Bhadra, Asoj, Kartik, Mangsir, Poush, Magh, Falgun, and Chaitra in mid-March. Exact boundaries move by a day or two each year, so check them against a patro.

**Plausible early crops**, each still an analogy: rice on wet ground; barley, wheat, and millet on dry ground; pulses; a winter oilseed. **[Gameplay invention]** covers any exact early mix.

**Post-Columbian (exclude from the early era):**
- **Maize.** [Chronicle tradition] Wright's vamshavali (p. 190) says that under Jagatjyoti Malla (dated by Desjardins & McCarthy to 1615–27), "some Indian corn (maize) was by chance brought from the east, mixed up among a quantity of mas or urd-dal". The "clever people" decided it "would cause a famine" and sent it back [2][25]. By 1793 Kirkpatrick lists "Muckhye (Indian corn)" as a main dry-land grain (1811, around pp. 94–95) [12]. That gives a 17th-c. arrival and an 18th-c. staple. It makes a good late-era event.
- **Potato.** [Archaeological] Domesticated in Peru (Spooner et al. 2005) [26]. Kirkpatrick reports that the people "could not raise even potatoes, without procuring every year from Patna fresh roots" (pp. 150–151) [12].
- **Chilli.** [Archaeological] Domesticated in Mexico (Kraft et al. 2014) [27]. By 1877 Wright lists "red pepper" and potatoes among the valley's winter crops (p. 46) [2].

**Irrigation.** [Later-period analogy: Licchavi] Licchavi texts mention canals (*tilamaka*), village-built canals rewarded by the king, and canal water divided into shares among village groups (no. 137 splits a watercourse seven ways) [11][9]. [Later-period analogy: Malla] In 1380 a canal at Yatapata, Bhaktapur, was repaired by a fortnight of voluntary labour and celebrated with "220 barrels of raw beer and 3 buffaloes" [1]. Royal canal institutions (*raj kulo*) should be treated as later; no source for the term was checked here.

June–September brings 1,176 mm, about 78% of the year's rain (FAO gives about 80% nationally) [18]. November and December together get 14 mm.

**Monsoon dates.** [Ethnographic, modern] DHM's figures for eastern Nepal [19]:
- Normal onset **13 June**, normal withdrawal **2 October**, a 112-day season.
- Extremes on record: shortest season 73 days (1979), longest 135 days (2025). Earliest onset 29 May 2025, latest 27 June 1982. Earliest withdrawal 2 September 1995, latest 19 October 2013.

These are **national (east-entry) dates, not Kathmandu-specific**. No Kathmandu-only onset normal was found.

**Frost and fog.** The January mean minimum is 2.8 °C, so frost on clear nights is plausible. The WMO sheet has **no frost-day or fog-day counts**, and none were found elsewhere. Leave them as tunable values rather than invented "data".

**Rivers.** The Bagmati drains the whole basin and leaves through the Chobhar gorge [2][23]. A 2022 flood study for the Bagmati Action Plan models these tributaries: Bishnumati, Dhobi Khola, Manohara, Hanumante, Nakkhu, Balkhu, plus Godavari and others. It places the Bagmati's source at Baghdwar (Shivapuri, about 2,690 m) [23]. Wright (1877) already names the Bagmati, Vishnumati, Nakkhu and "Rudramati, now called Dhobiyakhola" [2]. With a single outlet, a big storm floods the whole floor at once.

**Flood spike (real magnitude).** [Ethnographic, modern] DHM recorded these on 28 September 2024 [20][21]:
- Kathmandu Airport had **239.7 mm in 24 h**, beating its previous record of 177.0 mm (23 July 2002).
- The Bagmati at Khokana reached **6.16 m** against a 6.0 m danger level; the previous high was 22 July 2002.

ICIMOD reported over 340 mm in under three days, with 381.2 mm at Kumaltar, on ground already wet from a monsoon more than 25% above normal. It also blamed floodplain building [21]. So one bad day can deliver about two-thirds of a normal July. Floods cluster in July–August, but late-September spikes do happen.

**Drought.** [Chronicle tradition] The *Gopalarajavamsavali* records a three-year drought under King Ganadeva, ended by propitiating the Mahanaga, and later famines that killed "one-fourth of the population" [1]. [Ethnographic, modern] In 2024 the pre-monsoon (March–May) was about 20% below normal, and the heat stressed wheat and the valley's early vegetables [18]. In game terms, drought means a late or short monsoon, or a dry March–May.

|---|---|---|---|

| Yenya / Indra Jatra | Bhadra; pole raised Bhadra Shukla Dwadashi | End of the rains. Tradition credits Gunakamadeva (10th c.); Kumari Jatra added by Jayaprakash Malla. Vajracharya reads it as a rain and ancestor rite [29] | [Later-period analogy: Malla] |

| Tihar / Gai Tihar, Goru Puja | Kartik, five days | NTB: day 3 cow and Lakshmi, day 4 oxen and the Newar Mha Puja. Kathmandu Post (2025) reports cow, ox, and Govardhan puja on day 4, so practice varies [15][30] | [Ethnographic, modern] |
| Mata Tirtha | Baisakh 15, per Wright | A cowherd offers rice to his dead mother at a tank and her hand takes it [2] | [Chronicle tradition]; the only festival the chronicle ties to cowherds |

For a strictly early era, make the events agricultural (first rain, transplanting, harvest feast, canal repair) and add the named festivals as "later" layers.

## 9. Design cheat sheet: 12-month game year

Rain bins come from §6: very dry < 20 mm, low 20–70, building 70–150, heavy 150–300, peak > 300. Farm work comes from §5, herd from §4, risk from §6, festivals from §7. **Deterministic rule [Gameplay invention]:** month normals set the mean, and one seeded "year character" (onset date between 29 May and 27 June, season length 73–135 days, zero or one spike day up to 240 mm) sets each year. There is no per-tick randomness.

## 10. Open questions for a Nepali cultural reviewer

1. Should named Gopala kings (Bhumigupta or Bhuktamana) appear at all, or only "the cowherd people"? The two chronicles disagree.
2. Cow slaughter, buffalo feasts, and Dashain sacrifice: what may be shown, what may be a mechanic, and what must be off-screen?
3. Whose lake story should characters tell (Manjushri or Krishna), or neither?
4. Please check the readings of Regmi nos. 85 (*mahiṣīpīḍā*), 93 (what exactly is exempted), and 118 (*goyuddha-patākā*, a possible cattle- or bull-fight banner), and whether they are safe to cite.
5. Ask someone holding Verardi 1992 to confirm the Hadigaon radiocarbon date.
6. Are any valley archaeobotanical results unpublished or in Nepali-language reports?
7. Is there a Kathmandu-specific monsoon onset date, and are frost-day and fog-day counts available from DHM?
8. Exact Bikram Sambat month boundaries for the in-game calendar.
9. Who can supply licensable recordings of dhimay, dhime, and madal?
10. Could caste or guthi roles accidentally map onto unit types (herder, digger, priest)? Advise on neutral naming.
11. Is it respectful to make Mata Tirtha the game's oldest festival?
12. Should the FAO lentil row (spring-sown in the Hills) be replaced by local practice?
